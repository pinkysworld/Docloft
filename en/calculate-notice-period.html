Calculating a notice period: by when do I have to cancel?

To end a contract you need one date: your cancel-by date. It follows from the contract end date and the notice period. Here is how to work it out yourself and what to watch for.

The basic calculation

Cancel by = contract end minus notice period. If a contract ends on 31 December 2027 and the notice period is three months, you have to cancel by 30 September 2027. The calculator in Docloft shows exactly this example.

Where do I find the notice period?

Look in your contract, the terms and conditions or the order confirmation. Typical terms are “minimum term”, “renews automatically by” and “notice period”. Write down two things separately: when the current term ends and how long before that the notice has to be given.

Common pitfalls

  • Weeks, months, end of month: “three months to the end of the month” is not the same as “90 days”. Read the wording closely.
  • Automatic renewal: after a renewal a new term often starts with its own notice period. Count from the new end date.
  • Receipt by the provider: check the contract for the date by which your notice must reach the provider and which forms are allowed (letter, email, online form).
  • Weekends and holidays: leave a buffer of a few days instead of cancelling on the last day.

Plan a buffer

Put the cancel-by date in your calendar and set a reminder one or two weeks earlier. That leaves time to write the letter, reread the contract and send the notice in a way you can prove.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate the cancel-by date?

Subtract the notice period from the contract end. Example: contract end 31 December 2027 minus 3 months gives 30 September 2027. Your contract always takes precedence.

Where do I find my notice period?

In the contract, the terms and conditions or the order confirmation, often under “term” or “cancellation”. If unsure, ask the provider.

Does Docloft check whether my deadline is legally correct?

No. The app calculates with the values you enter. It does not check statutory periods or contract terms and offers no legal or tax advice.

This guide is general orientation, not legal advice. Your contract and the applicable rules are what count; if in doubt, ask a consumer advice centre or a lawyer.
